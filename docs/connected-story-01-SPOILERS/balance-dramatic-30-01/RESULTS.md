## Reader feedback

### Reader A

The cast has useful occupational distinctions: Monet compulsively calculates Friday payroll; Chip takes refuge in objects; Minnie asks for the music off; Robin refuses the assumption that she welcomes Grant’s death. Al’s care responsibilities and Tess’s inability to stop thinking about her gift glass make emotional stakes specific. However, thirty neatly shaped confessions repeatedly move from professional wrongdoing to personal insecurity to a named relationship. Vary speech length, interruption, evasiveness, and how much self-knowledge each speaker possesses. Several apologies explain motives more elegantly than frightened people would.

Repeated personal arcs lose friction when family top-ups, advisory refunds, sponsor refunds, restored labels and corrected damage reports already resolve misconduct before voting. Leave selected disputes unresolved and let characters disagree about the cost. Brie’s cheese bill is a slender lethal motive; Anne’s threatened school visit needs more concrete personal consequence if she is the solution. The physical glass timeline is useful, and bottle knowledge creates real distinctions, but many suspects simply attend the same preview. Give fewer characters shared access and more individualized movements.

The final questions routinely ask speakers to restate exhibit conclusions: Al compares his gold leaf against red paper, Drew decodes the studio sound, and Minnie explains both canceled hoods. Replace some repetitions with behavior, contested recollection or independently usable evidence. Anne’s final claim that the tour entered the donor room contradicts the docent’s completed note so directly that she becomes obvious only at the last checkpoint; motivate why she would make that checkable claim. Robin’s 'His death is not an answer either' and Faye’s ordinary half-inch direction to the donor are especially effective reactions.

### Reader B

The mystery is broad and initially balanced, then pivots sharply to Anne E. Dote. Her final 'The tour went through Grant’s private room' directly conflicts with the assistant docent's completed note. I would accuse Anne, although the positive poison-transfer evidence is less strong than the detected lie. Seed a smaller movement discrepancy earlier and give an independently witnessed private-room approach so the solve involves reconstruction rather than simply spotting the lone false restatement. Brie's clean flavoring container does not itself prove she added no separate poison; make clear why the evidence distinguishes Anne from the server.

Voices start vividly: Chip's touch prohibition, Monet's receipts and Frank's last-minute changes. Later rounds share a measured confessional cadence and 'Grant wanted/I wanted' structure. Keep each person's verbal habits through the investigative answers: Monet could interrupt with amounts, Saul evade via questions, Reed over-explain and Claire police vocabulary.

Personal details work best when attached to specific behavior: Anya's retained key, Tess's gift glass, Faye's 'half an inch,' Al wanting to call home. Spread these over the rounds instead of introducing almost everyone's private ache simultaneously. Give some characters denial, silence, anger or misplaced practical action rather than reflective self-diagnosis.

The procedural exhibits are useful and the service timeline is coherent. However, characters repeatedly translate each exhibit almost verbatim; trim third-round restatements already established by documents. Retain contested interpretations, particularly Barb's decision to remove power and Robin's refusal to treat death as restitution. Give the audience room to perform the deduction.
