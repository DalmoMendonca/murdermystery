# Blind review of C, releases 01–08

All eight releases were read individually in numeric order. Each result JSON was written and its existence verified before the following release was opened. No hidden selection, other version, or reviewer output was inspected. Scores are suspicion judgments, not probabilities.

## Overall plausibility

The core mechanism is understandable: a sealed hazardous original is stolen from forced storage; Grant has an identifiable empty glass available at reception; that glass alone tests positive; an intermediate small bottle could carry the poison. The independent storage alarm, canceled show, and ordinary reception service are distinguished clearly. A staged collapse provides a credible distraction without becoming the actual death mechanism.

The setting is an openly comic dinner-party game, so pun names and theatrical grievances are appropriate. However, the institution is implausibly saturated with simultaneous fraud, suppressed evidence, disputed title, blackmail, unauthorized alterations, and threats. Grant's assistant happens to possess decisive embarrassing paperwork for nearly everyone. This feels like the mystery distributing a secret to each player rather than a gala at which people happen to have competing agendas.

The historical poison source needs a little more grounding. The laboratory finding establishes compatible residue, but the narrative does not explain what condition the contents were in or how safe storage was accepted for a donor acquisition. The record's assertion that it remained hazardous is sufficient for the game premise, although the practical transfer mechanism remains thin.

## Natural speech

The introductions have good light jokes and easy performance hooks. Al's prompt-payment line, Chip's touching joke, and the director's immersive-experience joke are playable.

Later confessions share a much narrower voice. Nearly everyone admits a lesser offense, distinguishes it from Grant's greater offense, and finishes with a self-aware epigram. Examples include Chip's report being quoted forever, Frank's architectural specification, and the historian volunteering his own professional inadequacy. Individually these can work; across twenty-two people they make an improbable chorus of articulate ethical self-diagnosis.

Round 08 sounds especially like prewritten defense briefs: each speaker knows which exhibit or lab result resolves their assigned suspicion. The persistent attachment/file/receipt language reduces conversational heat. Few people interrupt, misunderstand, minimize, accuse a rival, or refuse an uncomfortable question. Give several characters shorter, emotionally partial answers and let documentary releases supply some of the precise distinctions.

## Early giveaways and fair planting

Release 02 clearly forecasts poison and the special glass, but it does not identify a culprit. The canceled collapse also anticipates a staged-death false lead. Those are fair method plants, though the density of procedural clarifications makes their later function visible.

At 06, Al and Brie become the most evidentially compelling suspects because they actually touch the glass. Their evidence is fairly planted before the positive glass test. Elon has a coherent anonymity grievance, but nothing released before 08 associates him with a small bottle or glass handling.

The small unmarked bottle arrives in 07, and Elon supplies a matching bottle action in 08. That makes him a reasonable final lead. It also makes the solution conspicuously late: the decisive association appears when everybody else's account is being defused. Earlier Elon details concern money and paperwork, so a reader cannot make the same positive bottle inference beforehand.

## Inference versus writing pattern

My final lead is Elon, scored 9, with Al 7 and Brie 6 still plausible. Elon's bottle beside the targeted glass is a stronger positive link than anyone else's remaining evidence. Threatened publicity supports a motive.

But the leap is not compulsory. His bottle is not expressly proven to be the residue-positive waste bottle; no witness sees him pour; no theft evidence identifies him. The language “wasn't part of my purchase” is curiously evasive yet voluntarily incriminating. A character trying to protect himself has little obvious reason to introduce that fact without being confronted with a witness or photograph.

The authorial pattern does significant work: twenty-one mostly explanatory statements surround one fresh incriminating admission. I would recognize the intended answer partly by that exception. It should not be treated as a forensic exclusion of all alternatives.

Post-theft entry receipts reduce Hugh and Tess's direct theft opportunities, not later possession or glass access. A document being legitimate or a threat referring to publication does not exclude its author's involvement elsewhere.

2. In the final release, confront him with a specific observation so his admission has a believable conversational trigger.
3. Connect the waste bottle to him through a distinctive, earlier-visible physical detail or corroborated disposal action; avoid relying on compatible residue alone.
4. Add genuinely independent glass-contact or location evidence if Al and Brie must be excluded. Do not present pre-fill contact as clearing Al.
5. Establish a theft-access timeline or another source of Elon's possession. Leave uncertainty honestly stated if the game only requires a strongest case.
6. Seed Anne's family connection and the other false-route objects earlier; cut explanations for suspicions the reader was never shown.
