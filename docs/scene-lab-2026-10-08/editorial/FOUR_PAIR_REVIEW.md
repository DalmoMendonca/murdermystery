# Independent four-pair review

Reviewer: scene_plausibility_editor. Read-only review of the exact inputs archived in
`four-pairs-reviewed-source`, with hashes in `four-pairs-manifest.json`.
This is an editorial rejection, not a blind suspicion test.

**REWORK this bounded prototype.** The agendas and innocent mitigations are usable, but the four pairs retain a conspicuous structural difference between innocent and guilty answers.

**Priority 1 — Method speech shape.** Every guilty answer introduces a detailed earlier encounter with the exact crime object, then redirects attention toward it. Every innocent answer briefly describes the disagreement and turns to explanatory documents. Hugh, Sue and Elle even repeat the engraved-name observation and demand to retain the same glass. Only one guilty branch is spoken, but that speaker can still stand out as the person suddenly narrating an elaborate poison-related encounter. This risk exists independently of whether players examine the contradicting evidence.

**Priority 2 — distinguish what the innocent facts accomplish.**

- **Hugh:** The surviving guarantee meaningfully weakens “he killed Grant to escape liability.” It makes death financially adverse rather than profitable. That is motive mitigation, not physical exclusion. The guilty confession preserves a credible retaliatory motive, although its rejection of financial self-interest appears principally in the ending.
- **Sue:** Her signed storage restriction rebuts the specific claim that her substitution authorized removal of the original. It cannot exclude unauthorized theft, particularly because the cabinet was forced. Her agenda and unauthorized paperwork substitution fit naturally.
- **Elle:** The pre-gala cancellation weakens “she needed Grant dead to recover ownership.” It also explains the concealed removal. Grant’s continuing appropriation and misleading credit remain credible grievances. This is useful motive mitigation, with no exclusion of glass access.
- **Ella:** The settled payment rebuts a suspected unpaid pledge or attempt to avoid paying the museum. Continuing repayment also defeats debt cancellation as a benefit. It barely weakens her explicitly stated motive: silencing threatened exposure of the financing. Describe that limited payoff accurately.

The guilty covers have understandable protective purposes and conflict with fixed stock or storage evidence. Ella’s sponsorship-demand scene is considerably more natural than exchanging a poison exhibit for her donation. No unavoidable shared-world contradiction appears in these four pairs.

The common Method question fits all four disputes, but the innocent answers mostly explain their legal or financial consequences rather than narrating what happened. The guilty answers answer it through scenes.

**Concrete repair:** revise these four Method pairs together so both branches describe comparable conversations and observations, with their documentary implications emerging from those accounts. Remove the repeated engraved-name/same-glass choreography. Preserve the existing specific innocent mitigations and challengeable guilty claims; adding more paperwork would not fix the asymmetry.
