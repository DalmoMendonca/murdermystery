## Cary Crates / t001

Accusation: Cary Crates

The bottle retained active cyanide, its closure was split behind, and a similar piece of foil-backed paper reached the poisoned individual glass. Clean shared punch makes contamination of that waiting glass plausible. Cary had staff access, visited the unattended place, handled its carton, and faced Grant exposing a deliberately false delivery report that threatened his livelihood. His final account specifically denies that the engraved glass was unpacked or consumed. That contradicts the manager's setup account, the picture of the bare engraved glass, and the photograph of Grant drinking from the crest glass. It also tries to detach the laboratory result from the actual murder vessel. Brie is the strongest alternative because she prepared the glass alone and washed her measure, but her ingredient container is clean and her account agrees with the retained flavoring results. Cary's specific false claim about an objectively documented vessel supplies the distinguishing evidence. It is an inference from deliberate misdirection rather than an exclusive physical identification.

The cast mostly sounds like different people under pressure: Chip is terse and controlling about objects, Monet thinks in wages, Cary speaks in clipped delivery problems, and Sue combines precision with personal concern. Small relationships and practical losses make the speeches warmer than a simple motive inventory. The bottle, glass and recording records offer usable factual checks, and Cary's final contradiction is available before voting. The solution is therefore reasonably fair, although opportunity alone remains shared and the decisive inference depends on treating his wrong explanation as calculated murder concealment. The repeated round structure feels deliberately assembled: nearly everybody first admits a compromising act and later explains its exact documentary correction. By the end, several speeches are close to reading an evidence caption aloud. The staff's collective decision to preview an unresolved poison bottle is startlingly negligent but acknowledged within the story. Offstage technicians, service staff and editors have clear evidentiary functions; I did not need an absent selectable speaker to answer a question. Some irrelevant family references add texture without confusing the central glass chain.

Post-vote: Cary's confession follows the existing vessel contradiction closely and confirms the inference I had already made. Taking poison during the preview and contaminating the waiting glass are newly admitted actions, but the accessible bottle, shared staff keys, empty cupboard bottle and his private-room visit already supported that route; they do not rescue an otherwise impossible solution. His fear of job loss was established, and the final moving-things line gives his professional identity a sad consequence. The confession still explains the evidence rather explicitly, but Brie's concern for staff transport and Artie's inability to deliver closing remarks give the ending useful human weight. It feels earned, though the physical case remains circumstantial until the confession.

- minor / naturalness: The final speech volunteers three linked technical denials about the boxed gift, substitute glass and tested vessel despite photographs contradicting all three; it feels tailored to mark the guilty answer. Suggested action: Let one pointed question about the crest glass elicit this denial, preserving the same factual contradiction.

- minor / pacing: The final round repeats the band construction and the full-staff versus shortened-press warning already documented in the previous release. Suggested action: Shorten the repeated documentary explanations and keep the personal consequences and interpersonal replies.

## Robin Banks / t002

Accusation: Robin Banks

The bottle held original active cyanide, was physically accessible at the preview Robin attended, and could later be reached with ordinary staff keys. Cyanide and similar red treatment-band paper in the individual glass connect bottle opening to the toast rather than the shared food or punch. The service account leaves the bare glass unattended between 6:32 and 7:04. Robin admitted a later petition visit to the set place, then asserted that the exchange and recording were public and denied approaching the waiting drink. The untrimmed interview directly contradicts this: the manager leaves the glass, Grant identifies his private room, and Robin interrupts there. This specifically conceals knowledge and access at the murder route. Brie is the strongest competitor because she prepared the glass alone and washed her measure. But she consistently admits those acts, while the clean ingredient container neither convicts nor clears her. Robin's documentary contradiction adds the discriminating evidence that Brie's opportunity alone lacks.

The mystery is solvable before voting: Robin's final location denial is checked by an unusually concrete recording, and the physical evidence supports poisoning an individual glass from the real bottle. The other accessible suspects prevent an early answer based simply on job title. I liked Monet's payroll pressure, Al's concern about care at home, and Sue and Mona's prickly affection; they give the room relationships beyond a list of grievances. Chip's clipped technical defensiveness and Justin's habit of improving stories are recognizable voices. However, many later speeches share the same rhythm of admission, personal wound, and direct address to another guest, so the final round sometimes feels composed as an ensemble confession exercise. The document release also spends considerable space disproving projections, replicas, and staging alternatives before those possibilities feel like live reader theories. Offstage staff supply understandable records and need not be selectable suspects. Robin's reversal is fair but conspicuous because it disputes both the recording and Robin's own earlier admission.

Post-vote: Robin's confession follows the previously available chain: the real poison bottle at preview, the unattended individual glass, the recorded private interview, and the false public-location denial. Taking poison during the preview and using the glass after the interview specify the execution, but they do not introduce a new decisive clue needed to identify Robin. The admission that the petition authorized neither violence nor speaking for the claimants gives the motive a credible moral aftermath. Chip and Brie receive humane exits consistent with their admitted misconduct and anger. The result feels earned, although the actual transfer remained inferred before voting and the final lie had already made the answer fairly conspicuous.
