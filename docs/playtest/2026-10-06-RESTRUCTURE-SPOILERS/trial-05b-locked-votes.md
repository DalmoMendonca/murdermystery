My working suspect changed from Brie to Sue before certainty arrived. Thus the final challenge is primarily joining two access exclusions across 22 people, rather than discovering a method. That is acceptable if the desired experience is a collaborative deduction, but the act title can imply a larger discovery than guests actually make. A more satisfying final accusation would name the one surviving guest and reconstruct a positive episode. Here Sue's folder route is positive source evidence, while her poisoning opportunity is only absence of an exclusion. Do not call this an eyewitness case.

The published facts require guests to accept mutually precise personal accounts, including the culprit's truthful route. The task explicitly says treat facts true, so I did. A live host should make that same epistemic rule unmistakable: scripted hearing facts are reliable; roleplay opinions and evasiveness are not additional forensic evidence. Otherwise the normal murder-party instinct that the murderer can simply lie undermines the elimination proof.

### Repeated proof forms
There are several different proof forms overall, but large batches repeat them: four people describe the unbroken crest, six people watch and feel their embossed original, three state WEST RETURN, and six have continuous-task accounts (split between source-window and glass-window exclusions). The six embossing confirmations in the last hearing are the most obvious repetition. Each adds a name to a class without adding a new deduction. The redundant sensory wording protects against carbon-copy loopholes, but after the second explanation its evidentiary purpose is unmistakable. The four film descriptions similarly advertise the alibi mechanism early.

Continuity clauses solve real fairness problems: the gantry release is out of reach, the mold needs both hands, the wall phone demands continuing responses, the fixed pedestal has live prompts, and the gate/manual release or glove ports occupy source-window suspects. These are stronger than weak 'I was working' alibis.
