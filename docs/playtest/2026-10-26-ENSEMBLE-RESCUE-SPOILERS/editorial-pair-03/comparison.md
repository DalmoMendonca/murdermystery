# Independent literary comparison

I read both complete manuscripts: all 22 roles, all 176 alternatives in each, and both performance procedures. This is an editorial comparison, not a solve trial. I used no other material.

**Preference: A, narrowly. Neither edition substantially improves whole-cast naturalness or dramatic variety over the other.** My scores are A: naturalness 7.3/10, arc variety 6.8/10; B: naturalness 7.1/10, arc variety 6.7/10. These are judgments of the dinner performance implied by the scripts, not measured audience results.

## What the room actually hears

Each round contains 22 answers: 21 innocent branches and one murderer branch, in a guest-selected order. Most opportunity-round changes to murderer branches therefore affect only one answer in any performance. Those edits cannot collectively transform the ensemble. The method round is effectively the same across editions, and almost all possible finale speeches are the same. Much of the good writing is shared.

A's strongest change is procedural. It announces the locked top three together, lets only their innocents speak, then always puts the murderer last. B proceeds in vote order, requires called innocents to finish even after a confession, and calls a fourth murderer only if needed. Both produce three or four speeches, never a parade of 22 confessions. A protects a small but real emotional effect: Sue's wish to go home with her friend, Vincent's invitation to Dada, or an innocent's practical next step happens before the room knows that friend is the killer. Under B those lines can arrive after the confession as though the speaker has not heard it. A's procedure resolves this without inventing dialogue. Its explicit no-fourth-innocent rule is also clearer. That is a worthwhile local improvement, not a rescue of the 66-answer performance.

## Global patterns, gains, and regressions

The shared voices are readily performable. Chip's imperatives, Monet's payroll fixation, Cary's clipped labor vocabulary, Frank's compulsive arranging, and Al's wish to call home give ordinary guests playable intentions. Specific human stakes—an unpaid cleaner, an earlier job lost with a coat still hanging up, someone needing help upstairs—are more effective than generalized anguish. Direct addresses establish relationships even when the chain jumps between groups. They are monologue addresses, however, not exchanges; the listener has no scripted opportunity to answer.

A improves some emphasis. Claire opens with Grant wanting her name on the claim, rather than leading with collection background. Vincent opens toward Dada, making the envy relational immediately. Brie's paid toast comes first, making the crucial distinction from the unpaid extra course easier to hear. These are modest staging and comprehension gains. Reordering existing facts alone earns little dramatic credit.

A also removes some conspicuously composed commentary: Art's murderer no longer delivers the stationery punch line; Elle no longer explains the borrowed-maker metaphor; Al's innocent answer ends with the concrete crew payment rather than a polished expression of remorse. Artie's murderer loses B's explicit self-commentary about tidying up after Grant. These changes help actors sound occupied by a problem rather than by the quality of their own speeches. But Artie also gains another present-tense explanation of his inability to host, and some A additions impose emotional finishes where B stopped sooner. Claire's 'I want to leave it there' and Art's repeated address to Dada plus refusal to explain the silence feel like written beat markers. B's simpler endings sometimes breathe better. Al's A murderer transition from moving heavy things to 'I needed paying' is less fluent than either a direct payment demand or B's metaphor.

The persistent sameness is deeper than sentence order. Most roles start with a legitimate professional grievance, acknowledge compromised conduct, defend a boundary, and finally convert professional identity into a statement about trust or shame. Even quite different offenses become versions of 'I was protecting something; I used someone; I must explain myself tomorrow.' Almost everyone is unusually articulate about their own psychological mechanism. This gives the cast moral seriousness but compresses its dramatic temperature. Defensive people, caring people, and status-conscious people too often arrive at similar measured self-knowledge.

Factual recurrence is not automatically a flaw. The prepared glass, the preview, contradictory labels, and intact evidence copies must remain audible for deduction. Shared questions and repeated denials are conventions of this scripted investigation. More troublesome is repeated emotional movement appended to those facts: the restrained confession of weakness, the named friend's appeal, the prospect of a difficult future conversation. That is not necessary to communicate the clue. Nor does the ensemble need random interruptions, grammatical damage, or additional jokes to disguise it. Its existing practical resistance could simply last longer in more roles.

Opportunity recurrence is most visible in the innocent answers that actually coexist: Artie, Monet, Dada, Barb, Elon, Elle, and Cary all have plausible business near the private place. This feels mechanically arranged, but these editions offer no basis to certify a safe factual cut. The much larger apparent procession of absent Grant / waiting glass in the murderer alternatives is not a performed procession and should not inflate the criticism.

## Remaining work and testing judgment

Material literary priorities are limited but real. First, reduce the tendency for everyone to identify their own failing in a complete, quotable sentence, especially in opportunity answers. Let selected practical refusals, vanity, or unresolved anger remain the ending. Preserve genuinely different exceptions already present: Monet's defiance, Brie's bill, Al's home responsibilities, Robin's outstanding demand. Second, smooth Al's A murderer transition; it currently sounds like two endings joined together. Third, keep A's innocent-before-murderer procedure coherent everywhere it is implemented. The supplied pair alone cannot verify host or private pages.

The similar method answers and explicit confession mechanics are genre conventions with useful factual work. The serious tone, puns in names, and limits on improvisation are not themselves must-fixes. I would not rewrite all finale alternatives merely because they resemble one another: only one murderer speech is heard, and only two or three innocents. I would still expect some formula to be audible in those few innocent speeches, but far less than in a complete editorial read.

**A full fair-play test is justified**, using the intended performance order and ordinary guests. These manuscripts contain enough specific relationships, resisted disclosures, and practical stakes to warrant testing; the literary shortcomings do not make a trial pointless. This is permission to gather evidence, not a declaration of fairness or a prediction that either edition solves correctly. The pair does not establish clue availability, balance across murderer choices, oral retention, or dinner pacing. A is my small editorial preference, particularly for finale sequencing and Reed's continuity. Calling it a substantial whole-cast improvement would overstate the observed change.
