# Independent whole-cast table-read assessment

Read in full: the ensemble manuscript only, all 22 roles and 176 alternatives. This assessment treats each performance as 21 innocent branches and one murderer branch. No character is assumed to know a private alternative.

## Overall experience

These people have playable wants, jobs and shame. The strongest moments involve someone doing something small while trying not to fall apart: Artie straightening Grant's chair, Frank still correcting furniture, Cary asking for anything that needs moving, Elon asking people to put their phones down. Ordinary guests can inhabit those actions without theatrical training. The museum's labor and ownership disputes feel connected rather than like unrelated costume-party motives.

Across the whole evening, however, the dominant experience becomes sequential deposition followed by sequential moral reckoning. Most characters get an Act I grievance, an Act II account of handling paperwork or visiting a place, an Act III defensive correction, then a finale explaining both evidence and personal wrongdoing. A few paragraphs are moving; 22 consecutive versions flatten one another. The material is substantially more natural at the level of one role than at the level of the room.

The all-innocent baseline contains 5,324 spoken words; replacing one role with its murderer alternatives adds a modest amount. At 120–140 words per minute that is roughly 39–45 minutes of scripted speech before questions, evidence handling, transitions or laughter. An extra response turn for every person would be expensive. Better exchange must mostly come from redistributing or trimming existing lines.

## Read-aloud rhythm and information load

There are genuine voice differences: Monet's wage urgency, Chip's clipped handling instructions, Sue's controlled irritation, Brie's practical commands, Justin's attraction to a headline. But those differences often submit to the same sentence machine. Witness the Act II murderer versions: Claire puts a copy beside the glass; Vincent leaves a release beside it; Reed puts a caption beside it; Penny puts a comparison beside it; Justin encounters “His glass was waiting. He wasn't.” Each is understandable alone. Together they turn the waiting glass into a refrain and make an extraordinary number of private errands sound author-arranged. Innocent Artie, Monet, Dada, Elon, Elle and Cary also describe access or proximity, so the refrain is not confined to a single guilty player.

Likewise, “Grant wanted…” followed by “I wanted…” efficiently establishes conflict, but repeated across Act I gives every grievance the same argumentative posture. The final reckonings repeatedly contrast the intended professional legacy with the crime: Reed's name “will be on this instead”; Justin's history becomes the invention he will be remembered for; Dada wanted attention to work and now gets attention to murder. Those are apt individual endings competing for the same emotional landing.

Listeners must separate the bowl's ownership label, bottle's press caption, intake condition, seller's attachment, frame report, sculpture publicity, pattern slides, program handout, running order and donor copy. Repeated words such as “correction,” “original,” “copy” and “label” do not always orient the ear. Claire's “Both files were on that desk” is useful; comparable object naming at selected transitions would help more than more exposition. Keep the bowl/bottle distinction audible. Justin's late Velvet Widow material is another conceptual arrival after audiences already carry the property settlement.

A number of speeches answer an absent challenger: “Vincent, don't try to make that sound kind”; “Reed, please stop calling everything I did a correction”; “Brie… You are treating two different errands as one visit.” Those are playable assertions, but they do not establish that Vincent, Reed or Brie has actually spoken that way tonight. They should not oblige the addressed guest to invent an explanation from another role's private script. “No, they haven't called” gives Penny an affecting interruption, but nobody has asked the question. “Production” in Frank's speeches is especially uncertain as a turn cue: no named player in this manuscript has that name.

The six grouped questions organize speaking well, but several are so tailored that they feel like headings for reports rather than things frightened guests ask. The biggest staging need is a clear rule about whether a named address hands over the floor. Without one, natural actors will answer and cautious actors will continue reading; the evening's pace will depend on personality rather than design.

## Finale compatibility and bounded repairs

The manuscript does not specify a finale speaking protocol. If the confession comes first, the incompatible innocent endings sound inattentive or callous. If it comes last, the earlier invitations can become tragic irony, but a confession interrupts promised repair and nobody gets to react. If the printed order governs, the problem changes with which of the 22 roles is guilty. A director cannot solve all worlds merely by telling players to be emotional.

Do not add 22 miniature aftermath speeches. Establish one explicit reveal order and provide very short conditional substitutions only for the future-facing promises above: Sue/Mona, Vincent/Dada, Dr. Fact/Dada, Elle/Chip, Monet/Artie, Brie/Monet and Justin/Anne are the clearest cases. Artie/Claire's staff handover also needs scrutiny. Preserve pleas that can intentionally remain painful; not every address requires an alternate ending.

Repair Reed's self-contradiction first. Then vary a bounded selection of Act II access accounts through existing behavior, deleting redundant “empty place / waiting glass” narration where its evidentiary job is already clear. Do not hide necessary access or add new events. Trim a few matching professional-legacy aphorisms so the remaining ones retain force. Replace unsupported conversational presuppositions with lines the speaker owns, or make a response cue explicit within the same allotted turn. A single short shared beat after the confession would buy more ensemble reality than a new round.

Preserve occupational diction, named objects at crucial transitions, mundane physical tasks, costs borne by staff and families, the distinction between advocacy and acting for claimants without consent, and the fact that innocent people remain culpable for consequential non-murder wrongdoing. Those make this a room of people. The principal repair is to let the room's public knowledge and emotional situation alter its closing behavior, rather than letting each private script finish in isolation.
