# Independent comparison

I read all 176 passages in each edition: all 22 roles, both alternatives, all three rounds and both possible finales. This is an editorial comparison, not a solve trial. I assessed a played room with 21 innocent branches and one murderer branch, then only the three selected finales and possible murderer fallback.

**Verdict: tie; no substantial improvement.** B offers several useful, small relational revisions. It does not substantially change either spoken naturalness or whole-cast arc variety. Most of the ensemble and all the murderer finales remain the same. The altered opportunity passages mostly reorder the same actions. I would selectively retain B's best changes rather than describe it as an ensemble rescue.

## What the room actually hears

Both editions already have playable individual voices. Monet's payroll fixation, Chip's handling orders, Frank's spatial fussiness, Dr. Fact's embarrassed courtship, and Cary's desire for another moving job give performers concrete behavior. Al's call home and Brie's responsibility for tired staff supply needs beyond reputation. Several short jokes arise naturally from occupations and relationships. Neither manuscript is merely a sequence of interchangeable speeches.

But the shared public structure remains conspicuous. Motives repeatedly run through Grant's demand, the speaker's disputed professional act, a threatened exposure/payment, then a compact personal explanation. Opportunity often becomes an errand report. Method repeatedly separates the true exhibit/service detail from the speaker's secondary dishonesty. The documentary vocabulary is necessarily useful, but “copy,” “query,” “attachment,” “approval,” “caption,” “invoice” and similar distinctions compete heavily for listeners' attention. Both editions sometimes make distressed people sound exceptionally good at explaining the thematic meaning of their own conduct.

This is not a claim that a room hears 22 confessions to poisoning. It does hear 22 motive statements, 22 logistical accounts and 22 method answers. Some innocent wrongdoing is already admitted during those rounds: Reed's malicious edit, Barb's false log, Al's bronze description and Anne's displaced workshop funding. Others maintain denials until an optional finale: Monet, Cary, Penny, Elle, Elon and Mona. That is genuine variation, but a large part of the cast still travels from defensive professional explanation toward admission and a promise to answer to somebody tomorrow. B barely changes that distribution. Most guests never deliver their revised final beat at all.

The common poison/glass explanation across the murderer alternatives is sensible functional repetition across mutually exclusive branches. It would be unfair to count all those endings as one performed sequence. The issue here is that B leaves each possible culprit's dramatic trajectory essentially untouched, while its major relational changes are reserved for a few optional innocent endings.

## Gains and regressions

B's strongest change is Elle accepting Chip's help packing. It answers her publicly spoken “let me finish rolling this myself” with an observable relaxation of control. That is a small but substantive character action, not merely smoother wording. Justin's “we'll start earlier” similarly turns the demand for fuller history into a practical collaboration with Anne. Sue explicitly choosing friendship over another contest is clearer than A's uncertain “I don't know what to do with that.” These add companionship to a finale pool otherwise rich in culpability, payment, corrections and future explanations.

There are costs. A's hesitant Dr. Fact invitation sounds like someone stumbling through an embarrassing moment; B's “one thing without a footnote” is more polished and more recognizably written as a closing line. B's Sue ending resolves uncertainty into a neat principle. B's Reed ending, “I removed a warning to finish a page,” loses A's explicit rejection of the space excuse and its renewed acknowledgment of wanting Grant humiliated. The earlier rounds still establish malice, so this is a weakening of the closing emphasis rather than erasure of the fact.

B's “Glass set out. Nobody home” for Cary has punch, but another absent-Grant/glass report is not new arc material. Some reordering reduces introductory context: Vincent's “The bottle came from a sale photograph” briefly leaves the referent looser than A's “I painted the bottle from a sale photograph.” These are minor delivery tradeoffs. There is no wholesale naturalness gain or collapse.

The social lines need to survive arbitrary culprit selection and finale order. B's Vincent tells Dada to bring paintings; Sue wishes to go home with Mona; Dr. Fact renews the dinner invitation to Dada. Those can land naturally before revelation. Since called innocents must finish even after a confession, they can also follow Dada's or Mona's admission of murder. B's firmer invitations then need an emotional interpretation the script does not supply. A has versions of this vulnerability too, especially the dinner invitation, but its uncertainty in Sue's ending is more accommodating. Elle can ask murderer Chip for help packing after his confession as well; that changes the emotional meaning of B's otherwise excellent reciprocal beat. This is a staging/relationship concern, not a requirement that every innocent anticipate the hidden branch. Nobody should act as though they heard a private alternative. The gains are conditional on what has actually been spoken.

Frank's new addresses to “Production” turn a recollected complaint into apparent direct dialogue with a collective that is not one of these 22 named roles. Staff may exist in the world, but this does not create a new played relationship within the supplied ensemble.

## Substantive or cosmetic?

Predominantly cosmetic, with a handful of substantive microchanges in optional innocent endings. A already supplies most of the distinct voices and outside obligations. B improves a few enacted relationships but does not redistribute the whole cast's revelations, conflicts or endpoints. The remaining major patterns are explanatory professional monologues, repeated document logistics, self-interpreting closing lines and too much dependence on optional finales for final emotional movement.

I would retain B's Elle and Justin ideas with delivery/order awareness, consider Brie's direct address, and keep A's more hesitant Dr. Fact. Neither edition earns a forensic-fairness certification from this prose comparison; that requires evidence and actual staged play.
